# Industry Evidence for RetailIQ

Research compiled 1 October 2026.

## How to read this file

Two hard filters were applied to everything below.

**Recency.** Only research published April 2025 or later. The publication date used is
that of the study itself, not of any article re-quoting it.

**Primary source.** A figure appears in the verified table only if the publishing
organisation's own page or PDF was fetched and the number read there directly. Every
verified entry carries a verbatim quote of the sentence containing the number, so the
wording can be checked against the source without trusting this file.

Both filters removed a great deal. Section 2 records what was removed and why, because
knowing that a famous number is stale or vendor-sourced is worth as much as the numbers
that survived.

A note on method. Several entries come from publishers whose credibility is not in doubt
but who did not state a method. Those are flagged. A figure with no disclosed sample,
panel or store count is weaker than one with, regardless of whose logo is on it.

### 1.1 Current out-of-stock levels, and what actually moves them

This is the most important entry in the file. It is peer reviewed, it is four months old,
and it says something your product needs to hear.

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Topic** | Out-of-stock levels in grocery and non-grocery consumables, and what drives on-shelf availability performance |
| **Figure** | Out-of-stock levels remain about 8 percent. The recommended boundary optimum target is 95 percent on-shelf availability |
| **Source** | Corsten, Gruen and LaBrecque, "Beyond technology: what differentiates on-shelf availability performance in retail", International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management (Emerald) |
| **Published** | 28 May 2026, DOI 10.1108/IJRDM-09-2025-0758 |
| **Method** | Stated in the abstract: survey data from 55 international retailers in the grocery and non-grocery consumables sector, assessing usage and implementation of five core shelf signals, namely gap scans, inventory records data, lost sales predictions, pick rate data and shelf images, analysed with a configurational approach. **The fieldwork period is not stated in the public abstract.** Flagged |
| **Verified** | Yes, Emerald abstract page fetched and the full abstract quoted |
| **URL** | https://doi.org/10.1108/IJRDM-09-2025-0758 |
| **Access** | Abstract public and free. Full text paywalled at £32 pay-per-view. The 8 percent and 95 percent figures are both visible in the public abstract, so a judge can verify them without paying |
| **Class** | **Peer reviewed.** The strongest evidence class in this file |

Verbatim, opening sentence of the abstract: "Retailers increasingly invest in
shelf-visibility technologies to improve on-shelf availability (OSA), yet the levels of
out-of-stocks in grocery and nongrocery retail consumables stubbornly remain about 8%."

Verbatim, closing sentence: "Recommending a 95% OSA boundary optimum target, the analysis
reveals that OSA performance emerges not from the adoption of any particular signaling
technology, but from the alignment and reinforcement of sensing, execution and empowerment
capabilities."

Three things follow from this, and the third one is awkward for RetailIQ.

First, there is now a current, citable out-of-stock rate. I had written this file's gaps
section saying no such number existed. It does, it is about 8 percent, and it is in a
2026 peer-reviewed paper with a stated sample.

Second, the number has not moved in twenty-four years. Corsten and Gruen's 2002 study
found 8.3 percent. Their 2026 paper finds about 8 percent. Same authors, new sample, new
method, same answer. The word they chose is "stubbornly". That is a far stronger premise
for your product than a single stockout statistic, because it says the problem has
survived a generation of technology aimed at it.

Third, and you should not look away from this: the paper's finding is that OSA performance
does **not** come from adopting any particular sensing technology. It comes from aligning
sensing with execution routines and frontline empowerment. A judge who reads this abstract
could use it against a shelf-vision demo. The right response is to agree with the paper and
show that you have built for it, which is what proposal 4.1 and the new proposal 4.8
address.

### 1.2 Shelf audits and inventory accuracy

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Topic** | Effect of physically auditing shelf stock on sales |
| **Figure** | An inventory audit produces an 11 percent store-wide sales lift |
| **Source** | Rekik, Oliva, Glock and Syntetos, "Inventory record inaccuracy in grocery retailing: Impact of promotions and product perishability, and targeted effect of audits" |
| **Published** | v1 May 2025, revised May 2026, current version June 2026 |
| **Method** | Approximately 24,000 SKUs across 11 stores, plus a field quasi-experiment on the marginal effect of stock counts on sales |
| **Verified** | Yes, arXiv abstract page fetched and quoted |
| **URL** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05357 |
| **Access** | Open, no paywall |
| **Class** | Academic working paper, **not yet peer reviewed**, no journal reference on the arXiv record |

Verbatim: "While performing an inventory audit is found to lead to an 11% store-wide
sales lift, the audit has heterogeneous effects with all the sales lift concentrated on
items exhibiting negative IRI (i.e., where system inventory is greater than actual
inventory). The benefits of inventory audits are also found to be more pronounced on
perishable items, that are associated with higher IRI levels."

Also verbatim from the same abstract: "We find that IRI is positively associated with
average inventory level, restocking frequency, and whether the item is perishable, and
negatively associated with promotional activity." And the authors' own framing: "reframes
stock counting as a sales-increasing strategy rather than a cost-intensive necessity."

This is the single most useful figure found. Caveat it honestly: it is a preprint, the
authors are established operations-management academics at Cardiff, Texas A&M and
Darmstadt, but it has not cleared peer review and 11 stores is a modest sample.

## 2. Unverified, stale or discredited. Do not use these.

This section is deliberately long. Most of the famous numbers in this field do not

### 2.1 Too old to use, even though they are real and well sourced

These are genuine, traceable figures. They are excluded only on date. If a judge asks
whether your evidence predates the post-pandemic channel shift and the arrival of GenAI
in commercial functions, every one of these would fail that question.

- **The 8.3 percent worldwide out-of-stock rate.** Real, and I verified it first-hand by
  extracting the text of the source PDF. It traces to Gruen, Corsten and Bharadwaj's 2002
  GMA, FMI and CIES worldwide study, restated in Gruen and Corsten's 2008 guide, which
  says verbatim: "retailers can sustain OOS reductions below the industry average of 8.3
  per cent" and "In our 2002 study, we found that to average 8.3 per cent." The data is
  from 2001 and 2002. Twenty-four years old. Do not use.
- **The shopper response to a gap.** Also verified first-hand from the same PDF, which
  gives buy item at another store 31 percent, substitute different brand 26 percent,
  substitute same brand 19 percent, delay purchase 15 percent, do not purchase item 9

  manufacturers "on average lose 30 per cent of the sales when consumers confront an OOS
  item". Excellent numbers, hopelessly out of date.
- **The backroom finding.** The 2008 guide states verbatim that "most OOS events are at
  the retail shelf or the store. In other words, the distribution center could have
  supplied, or did supply the item, but there was a problem in retail store processes or

  study. Same problem.

- **McKinsey Global Institute, 400 to 660 billion dollars a year for retail and CPG from

## 4. Proposals

Each proposal is tied to a specific figure above.

### 4.1 Make the audit itself the headline value, not the compliance score

**Evidence: KB-201, the 11 percent store-wide sales lift from an inventory audit.**

This is the most valuable thing the research turned up and it points somewhere slightly
different from where the product currently points. The finding is not that better
compliance raises sales. It is that *the act of counting* raises sales, because counting
finds items the system believes are on shelf and which are not. The lift was concentrated
entirely on negative-discrepancy items, and it was strongest on perishables.

Your app already has in-aisle capture and a planogram compliance audit. Right now those
are framed as scoring and policing. Reframe the output. After a shelf capture, the single
most useful thing to show the rep is not a compliance percentage, it is the list of SKUs
the system thinks are stocked but which are not physically on the shelf, ordered by
discrepancy. Call it what the research calls it, a phantom-stock list, and let the rep hand
it to the store manager. That is a concrete, cited, defensible reason for a rep to spend
four minutes photographing a bay, and it gives the rep something to give the buyer rather
than something to accuse them of.

The perishables finding sharpens this further. Your demo category is chilled protein. The
evidence says audit value is highest exactly there. Say so in the demo.

### 4.2 Lead the shelf argument with shopper-stated unavailability, not with out-of-stock theory

**Evidence: KB-203, 51 percent of Indian shoppers struggle to find healthier alternatives.**

The classic out-of-stock literature is unusable on date. But this NIQ figure does
something better than a stockout rate: it is the shopper saying, in 2025 Indian fieldwork,
that availability is the thing blocking a purchase they already want to make. Half of them.

Build the pitch generator's default availability argument on this rather than on a generic
out-of-stock statistic. The rhetorical shape is much stronger. The rep is not telling the
buyer their execution is poor, the rep is telling the buyer their own shoppers report not
finding the product. Pair it with KB-204, which establishes the demand intent at 49 and 37
percent, and the argument becomes: demand is stated, the barrier is stated, and the
barrier is one the buyer controls.

### 4.3 Add a channel-defence argument to the pitch generator

**Evidence: KB-205, KB-207, KB-208, KB-209, KB-210.**

There is a real and current story in the NIQ, Bain and Eternal numbers that your product
does not currently use at all. In Indian metros, modern trade share fell 2.8 points year
on year and modern trade volume fell 7.7 percent, while e-commerce volume grew 39.9
percent. E-commerce is now 18 percent of top-8-metro FMCG and over 21 percent in southern
metros, and more than three-quarters of that is quick commerce. NIQ's sharpest line is
that e-commerce "is already delivering more than half of the omnichannel growth", and
Eternal's own filings show the quick commerce business behind that growing net order value
121 to 137 percent year on year for three consecutive quarters while turning profitable.

The modern trade buyer your rep is talking to is losing share to a ten-minute delivery app.
That is the buyer's actual problem, and it is a problem that a well-executed, well-stocked,
eye-level physical shelf partly answers, because Bain's figure shows e-grocery is still
only 1.5 percent of total grocery. The shelf is not obsolete, it is under attack.

Add this as a pitch angle: "your category is leaking to quick commerce, and the defence is
availability and visibility in the bay." A rep who can open with a cited channel-shift
number is a rep the buyer takes seriously. Right now your pitch generator argues brand
economics. It should also be able to argue the buyer's channel position.

The Bengaluru setting makes KB-205 unusually apt, since southern metros are the highest
e-commerce-share segment in the NIQ data.

### 4.4 Reposition the whole product around the deployment gap

**Evidence: KB-209 at 18 percent scaling, KB-211 at 64 percent feeling ahead.**

Two credible firms, four months apart, with disclosed samples. BCG finds 18 percent of
CPGs scaling AI impact and 75 percent still in pilots. Deloitte finds 64 percent of CPG
executives believe they are ahead of their competition on agentic AI. BCG also finds that
more than half do not measure the ROI of their AI investments, which explains how both can
be true at once.

This is a better pitch opening than any productivity claim, and it is fully cited. The
problem in CPG AI is not capability, it is that nothing gets out of pilot, and the industry
does not know it because it is not measuring. Position RetailIQ against that: a tool a rep
can use on their next visit, that produces an artefact the rep hands to a buyer, with
measurable output. Not a platform, not a transformation.

Concretely, this argues for making the manager view sharper than it currently is. If the
industry's actual failure mode is unmeasured pilots, the feature that answers it is the one
that measures. Coverage and coaching are table stakes. What would differentiate is showing,
per rep and per store, what changed after the tool was used: discrepancies found, gaps
closed, orders taken. Even on synthetic data, having that view present says you understand
the real failure mode.

### 4.5 Soften two specific claims before a judge reads them

**Evidence: section 2.3.**

Your planogram config describes a "PicOS 5P retail execution standard, scored on OSA and
Share of Shelf", and the README repeats the framing. No standards body has defined either
term, the five Ps vary by source, and the "share of shelf should match market share" rule
has no research behind it and is contradicted by the space-elasticity evidence. Change
"standard" to "convention" and you keep all the credibility and lose the exposure. This
costs one word.

Similarly, do not put an accuracy number on the shelf-vision feature. Every available
figure is vendor marketing. The only independent measurement anyone has published is 87.75
percent, from a Taiwanese 7-Eleven deployment, and that is outside your date window and
paywalled. Claiming nothing is stronger than claiming a number you cannot source.

### 4.6 Reconsider the rehearsal feature's framing

**Evidence: section 2.2, the complete absence of credible evidence on AI role-play.**

I want to be straight about this one because it cuts against a feature you have built.
There is no credible published evidence that AI role-play or conversation simulation
improves sales performance. Every figure in circulation traces to a vendor or a
sales-training firm citing research it does not name. I looked for peer-reviewed work and
found only an unrelated medical-education study.

That does not make the feature bad. It makes the feature unevidenced. So do not let the
demo imply a measured benefit. Frame rehearsal as what it demonstrably is, a safe place to
practise before a real conversation, and let the judge decide if that is valuable. If
asked whether it works, the correct answer is that nobody has published evidence either
way, and saying that will land better than a vendor statistic.

### 4.7 Do not build a rep-time-saved claim

**Evidence: section 2.2 on Salesforce, and section 2.1 on Forrester.**

The obvious framing for a field tool is "reps only spend a third of their day selling, we
give the rest back." Resist it. The in-window figure is Salesforce's own, Salesforce sells
the software that fixes the problem it measures, and the series has moved 28 to 30 to 40
percent across three editions without explanation. The credible independent figure,
Forrester's 23 percent, is six years old. Neither is CPG field sales, and no non-vendor
measurement of CPG field rep time allocation appears to exist publicly at all.

If you want a time-saved claim, measure it on your own demo instead and label it as such.
A measured claim about your own prototype is more honest than a borrowed one about the
industry.

### 4.8 Answer the detection-versus-action objection before it is raised

**Evidence: KB-200, specifically the paper's conclusion rather than its headline number.**

This is the proposal I would act on first if you only act on one.

The strongest evidence in this file is also the most awkward. A May 2026 peer-reviewed
paper surveying 55 retailers concludes verbatim that "OSA performance emerges not from the
adoption of any particular signaling technology, but from the alignment and reinforcement
of sensing, execution and empowerment capabilities." It opens by noting that retailers keep
investing in shelf-visibility technology and out-of-stocks stay at about 8 percent anyway.

Your planogram compliance audit from shelf video is, in that paper's taxonomy, a sensing
capability. Shelf images are literally one of the five signals they studied. The paper says
sensing alone does not move the number. If a judge reads that abstract, the obvious
question is why RetailIQ would be different.

You have a good answer available but the product does not currently make it. The answer is
that RetailIQ is not only a sensing tool. The visit lifecycle you have built already spans
sensing (shelf capture), execution (suggested order, in-aisle capture, debrief) and
something adjacent to empowerment (the rep gets a grounded argument and a rehearsal before
walking in). That is the exact triad the paper names. Make that explicit. Put the three
words on the architecture slide, cite the paper, and say that the product is built around
the finding that detection without a routine and an empowered rep does not change the
shelf.

Concretely, this argues against treating the compliance audit as a standalone feature with
its own score screen. Every audit output should terminate in an action the rep can take in
the same visit: a line added to the suggested order, a talking point added to the pitch, or
a phantom-stock list handed to the store. An audit finding with no attached action is the
thing the paper says does not work.

Turning your biggest evidential vulnerability into your architecture rationale is worth
more than any statistic you could quote.

### 4.9 Be clear about what the rep is for, because the industry is answering that question

**Evidence: KB-211, AB InBev at 72 percent of revenue through B2B digital platforms.**

AB InBev's own audited FY2025 results say that 72 percent of its revenue now comes through
a B2B digital ordering platform, across more than 6 million customers, with 52.5 billion
dollars of GMV. Unilever's Q1 2025 filing says it completed a sales force divisionalisation
across its top 24 markets and is deploying digital selling tools.

Read that honestly. Order capture, which used to be a large part of what a field rep did,
is being absorbed by self-serve platforms at the largest CPG companies. A product whose
pitch is "we make the rep's order-taking visit more efficient" is building for a task that
is migrating away.

What does not migrate is the part a platform cannot do: standing in the aisle, seeing what
the planogram and the system both got wrong, and persuading a human buyer to change
something. That is the half of the visit your product actually addresses, and it is the
half with a future. Say so.

This also sharpens your suggested-order feature. Do not position it as the point of the
visit. Position it as the artefact the visit produces, downstream of the audit and the
conversation, which is where it genuinely adds something a B2B app does not. If anything,
the evidence suggests suggested-order is the feature you should de-emphasise relative to
the audit and the pitch, which is the opposite of where most field tools put their weight.

### 4.10 Make human-in-the-loop an explicit, cited design principle

**Evidence: KB-218, 61 percent prioritise human judgement over fully automated AI. KB-217,
11 percent have unified commercial operations. KB-219, 20 percent are growing revenue from
AI.**

Three separate figures, from EY's 850-executive survey and Deloitte's 3,235-leader survey,
point the same way, and together they argue against the direction most hackathon
submissions will take in October 2026.

A clear majority of CPG commercial leaders say they prefer human judgement to full
automation. Only 11 percent have the joined-up commercial function that a fully autonomous
agent would need in order to work. And only 20 percent of enterprises anywhere are yet
growing revenue from AI, against 66 percent who have got productivity out of it.

RetailIQ is already a human-in-the-loop product. The rep decides, the model prepares. Stop
treating that as a limitation of the prototype and state it as the design position, with
these three citations attached. The pitch is that an autonomous commercial agent has
nowhere to stand in an organisation where 89 percent of commercial functions are not
unified, and that the defensible unit of automation today is the individual rep's
preparation, not the decision.

This also gives you a clean answer to the inevitable "why not just make it agentic"
question. The answer is that 61 percent of your buyers say they do not want that, and you
can name the survey, the sample and the fieldwork dates.
